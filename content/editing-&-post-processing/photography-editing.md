# Photography Editing/Post-Processing

Photography editing and post-processing are the steps photographers take after capturing an image. Editing can be used to improve exposure, color, sharpness, composition, and other aspects of a photograph. Post-processing helps photographers create the final appearance they want while keeping the image looking natural or creating a specific artistic style.

## What Is Post-Processing?

Post-processing is the process of adjusting and improving a photograph after it has been taken. Photographers usually use editing software to make these changes.

Common post-processing adjustments include:

- Exposure
- Brightness
- Contrast
-  Highlights
- Shadows
- White balance
- Color
- Sharpness
- Noise reduction
- Cropping
- Straightening


The goal of post-processing is not always to make an image look completely different. Small adjustments can often improve the quality and appearance of a photograph.

## RAW vs. JPEG

The file format used when taking a photograph can affect how much editing can be done later.

### RAW

RAW files contain a large amount of image information captured by the camera. They provide photographers with more flexibility when adjusting exposure, white balance, shadows, and highlights.

**Advantages of RAW:**

- More editing flexibility
- Better recovery of highlights and shadows
- Greater control over white balance
- Useful for professional photography


**Disadvantages of RAW:**

- Larger file sizes
- Require editing software
- Usually need to be processed before sharing


### JPEG

JPEG files are processed by the camera and compressed to create a smaller file.

**Advantages of JPEG:**

- Smaller file size
- Easy to share
- Ready to use with little editing


**Disadvantages of JPEG:**

- Less editing flexibility
- More image information is lost through compression
- Extreme adjustments can reduce image quality


## Common Editing Adjustments

### Exposure

Exposure controls the overall brightness of an image.

If a photograph is too dark, increasing the exposure can make it brighter. If the photograph is too bright, decreasing the exposure can help recover detail.

### Contrast

Contrast controls the difference between light and dark areas.

Increasing contrast can make an image appear more dramatic, while decreasing contrast can create a softer appearance.

### Highlights

Highlights control the brightest parts of an image.

Reducing highlights can help recover detail in bright areas such as clouds or windows.

### Shadows

Shadows control the darker areas of an image.

Increasing shadows can reveal details in dark areas, while decreasing shadows can create deeper and more dramatic shadows.

### Whites and Blacks

The whites and blacks controls adjust the brightest whites and darkest blacks in an image.

These adjustments can help create a wider range of tones and improve the overall appearance of a photograph.

## White Balance

White balance controls the color temperature of a photograph.

Different light sources have different colors. For example, indoor lighting can appear warm or yellow, while some outdoor lighting can appear cooler or blue.

Common white balance settings include:

- Auto
- Daylight
- Cloudy
- Shade
- Tungsten
- Fluorescent
- Custom


Photographers can also manually adjust **temperature** and **tint** during post-processing.

## Color Editing

Color adjustments can change the mood and appearance of a photograph.

### Saturation

Saturation controls the intensity of all colors in an image.

Increasing saturation makes colors more intense. Decreasing saturation makes colors less intense.

### Vibrance

Vibrance increases the intensity of less-saturated colors while generally being more selective than saturation.

### HSL Adjustments

HSL stands for:

- **Hue** – changes the actual color
- **Saturation** – changes the intensity of a color
- **Luminance** – changes the brightness of a color


HSL adjustments allow photographers to edit specific colors without changing every color in the photograph.

## Cropping and Straightening

Cropping removes unwanted areas of an image and can improve composition.

Photographers may crop an image to:

- Remove distractions
- Improve framing
- Place greater attention on the subject
- Change the aspect ratio
- Improve composition


Straightening corrects a tilted horizon or other lines that should appear level.

## Sharpening

Sharpening increases the appearance of detail and edge definition in an image.

Sharpening can be useful for:

- Portraits
- Landscapes
- Product photography
- Architecture
- Wildlife


Too much sharpening can create an unnatural appearance or make digital artifacts more noticeable.

## Noise Reduction

Digital noise can appear as grainy or speckled areas in photographs, especially when images are taken at high ISO settings.

Noise reduction can help smooth the image and reduce unwanted noise.

However, excessive noise reduction can remove important details and make an image look overly smooth.

## Lens Corrections

Some editing programs include tools for correcting problems caused by camera lenses.

Lens correction can help fix:

- Distortion
- Vignetting
- Chromatic aberration
- Perspective issues


These corrections can be especially useful for architecture, landscapes, and wide-angle photographs.

## Local Adjustments

Local adjustments allow photographers to edit specific parts of an image instead of changing the entire photograph.

Examples include:

- Brightening a subject's face
- Darkening a distracting background
- Increasing detail in a specific area
- Changing the color of one part of an image


Common tools for local adjustments include:

- Adjustment brushes
- Radial filters
- Graduated filters
- Masks


## Retouching

Retouching involves removing or changing specific elements of a photograph.

Common examples include:

- Removing dust spots
- Removing distracting objects
- Reducing temporary blemishes
- Cleaning up backgrounds
- Removing sensor spots


Retouching should be used carefully, especially in portrait photography, so the image remains natural unless an intentionally edited style is desired.

## Layers and Masks

Many advanced editing programs use **layers** and **masks**.

### Layers

Layers allow photographers to make different edits separately without permanently changing the original image.

### Masks

Masks control where an adjustment is applied.

For example, a photographer could brighten a person's face without changing the brightness of the entire photograph.



## Non-Destructive Editing

Non-destructive editing allows photographers to make changes without permanently altering the original photograph.

This is useful because photographers can:

- Undo changes
- Try different editing styles
- Return to the original image
- Create multiple versions
- Make additional changes later

Working with RAW files and adjustment layers are common examples of non-destructive editing workflows.

|Photography Type|Common Editing Techniques|
|---|---|
|Portrait|Skin cleanup, exposure, color, sharpening|
|Landscape|Exposure, contrast, color, highlights, shadows|
|Wildlife|Cropping, sharpening, noise reduction|
|Sports|Exposure, contrast, cropping, sharpening|

|Street|Contrast, black and white, cropping|
|Macro|Sharpening, exposure, color, noise reduction|
|Travel|Exposure, white balance, color, cropping|

## Common Editing Mistakes

Beginners can sometimes over-edit photographs.

Common mistakes include:

- Excessive saturation
- Too much contrast
- Over-sharpening
- Excessive noise reduction
- Incorrect white balance
- Overusing HDR effects
- Removing too much natural texture
- Cropping too aggressively
- Making skin tones look unnatural

A good practice is to compare the edited photograph with the original and make sure the changes improve the image.

## Editing Software

There are many programs available for photography editing.

Common examples include:

- Adobe Lightroom
- Adobe Photoshop
- Capture One
- Affinity Photo
- GIMP
- Darktable

Some programs are designed primarily for organizing and developing photographs, while others provide more advanced image manipulation and retouching tools.

## Best Practices

- Always keep an original copy of important photographs.
- Use non-destructive editing when possible.
- Make exposure and white balance corrections before detailed edits.
- Avoid excessive saturation and sharpening.
- Zoom out regularly to evaluate the entire image.
- Compare the edited photograph with the original.
- Export photographs using settings appropriate for their intended use.
- Keep edited files organized and properly named.
- Use consistent editing styles for photographs from the same project.

## Key Terms

- **Post-Processing:** Editing a photograph after it has been captured.
- **RAW:** An image file containing extensive data captured by the camera.
- **JPEG:** A compressed image format commonly used for sharing.
- **Exposure:** The amount of light captured in a photograph.
- **White Balance:** Adjustment that controls the color appearance of an image.
- **Contrast:** The difference between light and dark areas.
- **Saturation:** The intensity of colors.
- **Sharpening:** Increasing the appearance of detail and edge definition.
- **Noise Reduction:** Reducing unwanted digital noise.
- **Cropping:** Removing part of an image to improve framing or composition.
- **Retouching:** Editing specific areas or objects within an image.
- **Mask:** A tool that controls where an adjustment is applied.
- **Non-Destructive Editing:** Editing that preserves the original image.


## Summary

Editing and post-processing are important parts of modern photography. Photographers can use editing software to correct exposure, adjust colors, improve composition, reduce noise, sharpen details, and remove distractions. Understanding how to edit photographs effectively allows photographers to improve their images while maintaining a natural appearance or creating a specific artistic style. A good editing workflow should protect the original photograph, use adjustments carefully, and prepare the final image for its intended purpose.
